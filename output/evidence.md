# Evidence

Run 2026-09-25, on data through the 2026-09-24 close.
Source: Yahoo Finance daily OHLCV via yfinance. 178 of 179 tickers; X did not download.
Sample: 76 candidates across 46 Thursdays, 2024-08-15 to 2026-09-24.
Outcome: "goes again" = the next session closes above the highest high of the
consolidation days. The next session is normally Friday; in a holiday week it is the
next trading day.

Tables are generated by `src/evidence.py`; the read-throughs are mine. The control line
under "Comparison" comes from `control_check.py`.

## The short version

The lean does separate: names it likes clear their range about three times as often as
the ones it doesn't. But the whole candidate pool clears its range less often than an
average name does on an average Thursday, because the bar is much further away for these
names. The setup produces mild continuation, not breakouts.

One note before the tables. I first ran this on a panel that had silently downloaded 50
of 179 tickers after Yahoo rate-limited me, and on that sample the lean looked like it
did nothing. Fixing the download reversed that. Everything below is the full universe,
and there is now a coverage check in `src/data.py` that stops a run rather than screening
a quarter of the names.

**Read:** 58% up against a 52% base is about one standard error on 76 rows, so treat it
as nothing on its own. The median next-day return is five times the universe median,
which is more interesting, but these are medians on a small, clustered sample.

## By lean

| lean          |   n | go_rate   | median_next_return   | median_excess_return   | up_rate   |
|:--------------|----:|:----------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|:----------|
| unclear       |  24 | 4%        | +0.42%               | +0.34%                 | 54%       |
| leaning go    |  50 | 14%       | +0.67%               | +0.09%                 | 60%       |
| leaning stall |   2 | 0%        | -1.92%               | -2.01%                 | 50%       |

**Read: the lean separates, but only on the go rate and only weakly.** 14% against 4% is
a 10 point gap with a standard error of about 6 points, so roughly 1.6 standard errors.
Directionally supportive, not established. Note that it does not separate on returns:
median excess return is actually lower for leaning go than for unclear. So the lean picks
names that clear their range, not names that pay more the next day.

The score is also compressed upward. Only 2 of 76 candidates ever scored leaning stall,
because two of the five features, volume dry-up and gain retained, are close to the entry
filters themselves. A name cannot be a candidate unless it already faded volume and held
the move, so those two are positive almost every time and push the score up before the
other three vote. The fix is to keep both as filters and drop them from the score.

**Read:** this is the cleanest result in the file once you put it next to the base rates
below. Two-day setups go again 20% of the time against a 19.7% unconditional rate, so
they match. Three and four-day setups go 4% against 16.8% and 14.8%, so they are far
below. The mechanical explanation fits: every extra day widens the range, so the breakout
level sits further above Thursday's close. If I kept one thing from this study it would
be to restrict the screen to two-day consolidations.

## Comparison: no screen at all

- Any screened name, any Thursday, next-session up rate: 52% (n=19,293)
- Median next-session return, same population: +0.10%
- SPY next-session up rate: 55% (n=109)
- Same "goes again" test on every name on every Thursday: 19.7% at two days, 16.8% at
  three, 14.9% at four. Weighted to the candidates' mix of window lengths (30 / 23 / 23):
  **17.3%** (n=57,525). The screen's 11% is below that, by about 1.8 standard errors.

**Read:** on its own the 11% go rate reads as a failure, and I would rather show that
than bury it. But the comparison is not apples to apples. For a quiet name the trailing
three-day high sits maybe 1% overhead; for a name three days after a 17% move on 3x
volume it can sit 5 to 10% above the close. ARM on tonight's list needs a 10% Friday to
count as a go. So part of the gap is my outcome definition punishing exactly the names
the screen is built to find.

Both results together are the actual finding: these names drift up slightly more often
than average and clear their own range less often. Mild continuation, not breakouts.

For the next version: normalize the bar, either as a next-day move of some fraction of a
daily sigma, or by comparing each candidate against control names matched on
distance-to-high in sigmas rather than against the whole universe.

## A live example of what the screen cannot see

Tonight's top-scoring name, WBD at +0.93, is not a momentum setup at all. Warner Bros.
Discovery is under an agreed cash takeover, and the 10.8% move on 15.7x volume was a
reprice on news that a legal obstacle to the deal was clearing. It then sat in a range
0.6% wide just under the deal price.

That is why it scored so well. The lean rewards a volatility collapse, and deal pinning
is the most complete volatility collapse available. It is also why the name cannot go
again in any meaningful way: the acquirer's price is the ceiling.

The general lesson is that three of my five features can be maximized by a mechanical
state that has nothing to do with the psychology the PM trades. A merger-arb filter, or
simply flagging names with extreme range contraction near a round price, would catch it.
Until then the screen needs a human to read the names, which is how it should be used
anyway.

like that. I am still not taking the 4x setting: it is 37 names, its 14% remains below
the 17.3% base rate, and picking the best cell after the fact is how you fool yourself.

## Traps in this check, named

1. **Survivorship.** The universe is today's liquid names, so anything delisted or
   collapsed is missing. The check is biased toward the survivors of the period.
2. **Look-ahead.** Features use only rows dated on or before the screen date, and
   trailing statistics are shifted so a day is never judged against a window containing
   itself. The outcome is computed in a separate function and never feeds the lean.
   A test truncates the future and asserts nothing changes. One residual risk: Yahoo's
   adjusted prices are today's adjustments, not what was on the tape that evening.
3. **Thresholds fitted to the answer.** The numbers in config.py came from how the PM
   described the setup and were left alone. The grid shows nine combinations, which is
   the whole count.
4. **Overlapping and clustered events.** Candidates cluster into earnings season and into
   a handful of strong weeks, so the Fridays are not independent draws. The effective
   sample is closer to the 46 distinct Thursdays than to 76 rows.
5. **Sample size.** A few percentage points between buckets is inside the noise. Direction
   only.
6. **An unmatched control.** The 17.3% base rate runs the same test on names that are not
   comparable, as described above. Right first comparison, wrong last one.
7. **Corporate actions.** Nothing here identifies deals, halts or splits. WBD above is the
   live example.
8. **No costs.** Nothing subtracts spread or impact, and these names are wide the morning
   after an event.
9. **Split-adjusted prices, unadjusted volume.** Yahoo back-adjusts OHLC but leaves volume
   raw, so for up to 20 sessions after a split the volume ratio is measured against a
   median from a different share count. A name can register a false impulse.
10. **One missing ticker.** X did not download, so the universe is 178 names.

## What free daily data cannot test, and what I would use instead

- **Was the excitement real flow or a squeeze?** Daily bars cannot separate them. With
  intraday prints and short interest or borrow data I would split candidates by whether
  the event day was accumulation or covering, which I expect matters more than any of the
  price features here.
- **Was the move fundamental, or a deal?** A point-in-time news and corporate actions feed
  would separate guide-ups, upgrades and mergers, which the screen currently treats
  identically. WBD shows what that costs.
- **Did it break out cleanly?** Minute bars would let the outcome distinguish a close above
  the range from a wick and fade.
- **Was the setup crowded?** Options open interest and positioning data would say whether
  everyone else is looking at the same list.
